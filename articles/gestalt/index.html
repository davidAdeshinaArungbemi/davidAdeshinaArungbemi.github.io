Gestalt: The Whole is not the Sum of Its Parts

An addition to the previous article, “I Tried to Build an Aesthetic Model of the Human Brain. I Failed. Here’s What I Learned.”

Suzume · David Adeshina Arungbemi

Can different combinations of parts produce the same whole? If so, how many such combinations are possible?

Introduction

It is my belief that multiple visuals (e.g. artworks, images) can produce the same perceptual experience. They may differ in fine-grained detail — the exact placement of elements, the precise texture of a surface — but the experience they produce can still be approximately the same.

This belief is compressive in nature. It suggests that while artworks exist in a high-dimensional space of form, where small changes in structure can produce infinitely many variations, perceptual experience is not equally high-dimensional. Perception acts as a kind of compression: rich visual detail is reduced into a smaller set of felt qualities. Many points in the space of possible images can map onto the same region in the space of experience.

This connects closely to what Gestalt psychologists observed: the mind does not register isolated elements first. It organizes sensory input into structured wholes — balance, tension, figure-ground relationships — and these organized wholes are what perception delivers to us. The parts come later, if at all. What matters here is the distinction between this organizational process and what follows from it: the aesthetic response, the affective charge, the sense that an image has a particular presence. Gestalt is the structure that gets handed to experience. The two are related but not the same thing.

Two different visuals can differ at the level of their physical makeup while converging at the level of their perceptual organization — the structured whole that the visual system constructs from them. And when that organized whole is similar enough, the experience that follows can be similar too: similar tension, similar emotional direction, similar sense of harmony or disruption.

So the key claim is not that different artworks are identical in what they contain, but that they can be equivalent in how they are organized perceptually, and that this organizational equivalence can produce structurally similar experiences. That is what this experiment tries to test.

My Gestalt Experience

When I go on Pinterest and see a piece of photography or art for the first time, I immediately feel something from the whole of the image, not from any particular part of it.

It is not that I cannot see the parts. I can notice details, structure, composition, even mistakes, if I focus on them. But my attention is not primarily organised around those parts. It is drawn toward the overall organization of the image as a single field — the way elements group together, the weight and balance of the composition.

The whole of the image arrives first. Because of this, I often miss things that a more analytically focused viewer might catch immediately — unusual hand placements, subtle anatomical inconsistencies, or the fact that an image is not perfectly symmetrical if you flip it along the vertical axis. In the moment of viewing, those details do not define the encounter. What I am anchored to is the structural organization of the image as a whole: how it holds together.

The aesthetic response — the mood, the atmosphere, what you might call the presence of the image — comes from that organized whole, not from any individual part. I think this is how most people encounter images, even those trained to analyze them. The first encounter is almost always organizational before it is evaluative.

Measuring Gestalt

Measuring Gestalt organization is difficult precisely because Gestalt is not a property of isolated parts. It is a property of how parts are organized into a whole in perception — something that exists at the level of the structured percept rather than at the level of any single measurable feature. You cannot point to the Gestalt of an image the way you can point to its color or its size. But it can be studied, not measured directly, but triangulated from multiple overlapping signals.

The most direct approach is through subjective reports: participants describe or rate their experience of a stimulus, how similar two images feel. The limitation is that language is imprecise and inconsistent across observers. But patterns emerge across many responses that point toward stable perceptual groupings — not reducible to any single physical feature of the images being compared.

A complementary signal comes from brain activity. In neuroaesthetics, researchers compare neural activation patterns while participants view different stimuli. When physically distinct images evoke similar responses in the visual cortex, it suggests that the brain is encoding them as structurally similar at the level of perceptual organization, despite differences in their low-level features.

Crucially, not all neural similarity reflects perceptual structure. Activation in affective regions of the brain corresponds to evaluative responses — how a stimulus is felt or judged — rather than how it is organized perceptually. These signals arise downstream of perceptual processing and should be treated as distinct from the representation of Gestalt structure itself.

The third approach is through embedding spaces in machine learning models, and it is the most relevant one to this article. Models such as CLIP map images into high-dimensional vectors where distance reflects semantic similarity — how close two images are in terms of what they depict and what concepts they are associated with. This is a useful proxy for one dimension of perceptual organization: categorical content, semantic meaning. But it is not a complete proxy for Gestalt, because Gestalt is primarily about structural organization rather than semantic content.

This was a problem I ran into while building this experiment. CLIP encodes what an image means in the semantic sense. What it does not strongly encode is how an image is visually organized — the arrangement of edges and textures, the spatial groupings, the figure-ground relationships that the visual system imposes before it identifies anything categorically. Gestalt operates precisely at this organizational level, which is prior to semantic interpretation. An approach that only uses semantic similarity is missing the dimension where Gestalt most directly operates.

This is what brought DINO into the experiment. DINO is a self-supervised model trained on images alone, without category labels or language supervision. Because it never learned what things are called, it learned what things look like structurally — edge arrangements, textures, spatial organization. Two images that are organized similarly will be close in DINO space even if they depict entirely different subjects. DINO is therefore a proxy for structural organization in the Gestalt sense, while CLIP is a proxy for semantic content. Together they begin to triangulate what Gestalt describes: not just what the image means, but how it is organized as a visual whole, prior to meaning.

A third dimension — the aesthetic response that arises from that organized whole — is captured separately by the NIMA model, which is trained on human aesthetic ratings. These are three genuinely distinct signals, and keeping them distinct is the conceptual core of the experiment.

Can different combinations of parts produce the same whole?

This idea has a strong foundation in philosophy and cognitive science under the concept of multiple realisability — the observation that many different underlying structures can give rise to the same higher-level property. Perception itself is a familiar example: very different patterns of light on the retina can produce the same perceived object, because the visual system groups inputs into stable forms based on relational structure rather than exact component identity. The whole is not fixed by its specific parts, but by how those parts are organized in relation to each other.

In philosophy of mind, this extends into the claim that mental states can be implemented in many different physical arrangements, provided the functional or relational structure is preserved. Applied to perceptual organization, this suggests that multiple distinct artworks — differing in exact composition, texture, or fine detail — can still produce the same organized percept, because what the visual system responds to is not the individual elements but the configuration of relations they form: the balance, the spatial groupings, the structural tensions.

So yes: different combinations of parts can produce the same whole, but only when “whole” is understood as an emergent structure defined by how components are organized in relation to each other, not as a fixed sum of those components. The implication is that the organized percept — what Gestalt psychology calls the whole — is not uniquely tied to its specific parts. It is a stable pattern that can be reached through many different underlying arrangements. That is the philosophical setup. The experiment is an attempt to test whether it holds computationally.

Computer Simulation

The simulation uses three signals to constrain what counts as “the same whole,” each capturing a genuinely different dimension of the target image.

The first is CLIP similarity, measuring semantic content — what the image depicts, in the representational sense that a vision-language model encodes. The second is DINO similarity, measuring structural organization — how the image is visually arranged, independent of what it depicts categorically. These two together address the perceptual whole: what the image is organized as, and what it means. The third is the AVA aesthetic distribution, which captures something different again — not the perceptual structure of the image, but the spread of human aesthetic responses to it. This is not a single score but the full distributional profile of how different observers rated the image, from 1 to 10.

A key assumption here is that the AVA distribution, while not representative of every possible observer, acts as a useful proxy for the diversity of human aesthetic response. It does not claim universality, but it captures the spread of differences in how people evaluate a visual stimulus, making it a statistical model of subjective variation rather than a fixed truth. Two images can both average a rating of 6 while having completely different distributional shapes — one with votes clustered tightly around the mean, another spread broadly across the range. The simulation treats these as distinct and measures the difference accordingly.

In this framing, DINO and CLIP constrain the perceptual whole — the structural and semantic organization of the image. The AVA distribution constrains the aesthetic response that arises from it. These are the actual objectives of the optimization, each something the system is genuinely trying to minimize, weighted dynamically using multi-task uncertainty weighting. The goal is not to produce an image that scores well on any single one of them, but to find an image that satisfies all three simultaneously — and to do this from multiple random starting points, to see whether they land in the same place.

What we optimized, and why it changed

The first version of this experiment optimized the noise latent directly — the tensor a diffusion model starts from before denoising. The noise is what gets transformed into the image, so optimizing it seems like a natural way to steer the output. The problem is that noise latents are unpredictable to optimize. Small changes produce large and discontinuous changes in the output, which makes gradient-based optimization unstable. The loss would improve for several steps and then the image would abruptly shift into something entirely unrelated to the target.

The solution was to move the optimization into text embedding space. SD-Turbo, like most modern diffusion models, is conditioned on a text embedding — a vector representing the prompt. By adding a small learnable delta to the unconditional (empty prompt) embedding, the model’s generation can be steered without any text description at all and without modifying the model’s weights. The optimization space is smoother, the gradients are more informative, and the relationship between the variable being optimized and the output image is more consistent. This change — from optimizing the noise latent to optimizing a text embedding delta — was one of the most consequential decisions in the project.

Building the loss function

The first working version of the experiment used only CLIP and NIMA. The CLIP loss pulled generated images toward the target’s semantic representation. NIMA provided the aesthetic distribution signal. This was enough to produce images that were semantically recognizable as related to the target and aesthetically in the right territory, but structurally inconsistent — different random seeds would arrive at images that felt related in content while looking quite different visually. The missing piece was structural organization, and adding DINO resolved this substantially.

CLIP ViT-L/14 provides the semantic signal. An earlier version used ViT-B/32, which is lighter but less expressive; switching to the larger model improved the consistency of semantic convergence across trajectories. DINO ViT-S/8 provides structural similarity, encoding spatial arrangement and texture relationships independent of categorical identity. NIMA outputs a 10-bin probability distribution over aesthetic ratings for any input image, and the loss used is Jensen-Shannon divergence between the generated image’s predicted distribution and the target’s actual distribution. An earlier version used KL divergence; JSD is symmetric and better behaved numerically here.

These three losses are the objectives of the optimization — co-equal targets the system is genuinely trying to minimize together, not as regularisers. Their weights are assigned dynamically using the multi-task uncertainty weighting scheme from Kendall and Gal (2018): each loss receives higher weight as the optimizer becomes more confident that objective is achievable. By the end of training, all three task weights are at or near their maximums, meaning the system has committed fully to each simultaneously.

Why the losses need to be introduced in sequence

When all three losses are active from step one, NIMA stalls. In the version of the experiment that tried this, it plateaued at a Jensen-Shannon divergence of 0.0109 by step 256 and showed no further improvement across the remaining training steps.

The reason is one of prerequisite. NIMA’s distributional gradient is a signal over the aesthetic properties of an image that already has recognizable structure and content. It can refine the aesthetic character of an image that has already established what it is. It cannot usefully guide a noise pattern that has not yet established any visual organization, because aesthetic response is a response to something, and before that something is perceptually present, the gradient has nowhere coherent to point.

The solution is a three-phase curriculum. Steps 1 to 100 optimize only the DINO loss: structural organization is built first, with the full gradient budget committed to establishing spatial and textural relationships. Steps 101 to 400 add CLIP: semantic content enters once there is structural organization to build meaning on top of. From step 401 onward, NIMA enters into a generative basin that already has structure and semantic content, and the aesthetic distribution tightens from there.

This ordering reflects the same dependency that visual processing itself follows — structural grouping before categorical identification, perceptual organization before aesthetic evaluation. The curriculum respects those dependencies rather than treating all three constraints as equally applicable from the start.

The loss curves confirm this. DINO drops from near 1.0 at initialization to around 0.1 to 0.2 by step 100, across all targets. When CLIP enters at step 101, a brief spike in CLIP loss appears — DINO has been pushing the embedding in one direction for 100 steps, and CLIP initially disagrees with that direction. Within a few hundred steps, they align and descend together through phase two. This spike appears in every target, in every run, without exception. It is not a failure. It is what two loss signals look like when they are working out a shared direction after entering at different times. By step 401, when NIMA enters, DINO and CLIP losses are both near their convergence values, and the aesthetic distribution tightens steadily through phase three.

Results

Five target images were selected from the AVA dataset, spanning different visual registers: an interior gallery scene with a figure contemplating a large abstract painting (857228), dark grass silhouettes against an orange sunset (228928), a rural hillside with wooden fence posts (33753), a dense field of red mineral crystals (854398), and a black-and-white photograph of a person in overgrown grass (518216). For each target, three independent optimization trajectories ran from different random seeds to 3000 steps.

Final DINO cosine distance is under 0.005 across all fifteen trajectories. Final CLIP cosine distance is between 0.001 and 0.011. Both are near-zero, meaning the generated images match their targets at both structural and semantic levels. The NIMA Jensen-Shannon divergence at convergence:

JSD ranges from 0 (perfect distributional match) to 0.693 (maximum divergence). These values are consistently tight across seeds — the generated images match not just the rough aesthetic territory of each target but the precise shape of human response to it.

The more important result is not the final values themselves. It is the consistency with which they are reached from different starting points.

The mineral crystal target (854398) converges faster and more completely than any other. By step 512, the three outputs are already visually indistinguishable from each other and from the general character of the target. The minerals have strong structural repetition and a dominant, specific color, which makes all three constraints simultaneously satisfiable earlier in the optimization than targets with more compositional complexity.

The black-and-white image (518216) provides the clearest test of the system’s limits. The trajectories move toward the right region — desaturated tones, organic textures, figures in open space — but fail to converge as cleanly as in the other cases. One path lingers in crowd scenes without resolving into a single figure; another drifts toward animals in grass rather than a person.

Notably, embedding distances in DINO and CLIP remain favorable, suggesting that structural and semantic similarities are captured at the representational level. Yet the visual outputs tell a different story. This divergence highlights a gap between embedding alignment and perceptual outcome.

The result suggests that this image’s Gestalt is harder to recover with the computational tools. Its defining features — a single figure, muted tonality, and sparse spatial composition — may act as subtler constraints than the more dominant cues of color or texture seen in other examples.

Contrastive Verification

To test whether convergence reflects target-specific structure rather than general aesthetic quality, the optimized images were compared to randomly sampled AVA images with the same mean aesthetic score. The question was whether images optimized toward the same target are more similar to each other than images that are merely similar in aesthetic rating.

All five targets supported this hypothesis. Using DINO, mean pairwise similarity between converged images was ~0.99, compared to 0.05–0.13 for the random baseline. This indicates that optimized images share highly consistent structural organization, while same-score images do not.

Using LPIPS, converged images were also perceptually closer (0.55–0.70) than the baseline (0.75–0.90), though not identical. This remaining variation suggests that convergence occurs at the level of structure rather than exact visual detail.

Together, these results indicate that the optimizer is capturing something specific to each target’s Gestalt, rather than simply producing generally high-scoring images.

Conclusion

The question at the start of this article was whether different combinations of parts can produce the same whole. The results suggest that they can — consistently, across five targets and multiple independent runs, under three simultaneous constraints.

From random initializations, each trajectory converges to the same region of representation space. Similarity in DINO reaches ~0.99, aesthetic distributions match closely (JSD 0.002–0.009), and the resulting images share structural organization in a way that random images of comparable quality do not. The system is not simply producing “good images” — it is converging on something specific.

This supports a compressive view of perception: high-dimensional image space is reduced into organized percepts, and aesthetic response is anchored to these structures. When structural, semantic, and aesthetic constraints are jointly satisfied, the solution does not collapse to a single image, but to a narrow neighborhood. Within that space, variation persists at the level of fine detail, while higher-order organization remains stable.

One implication complicates an earlier assumption. Images that produce the same percept are not arbitrarily different in structure and composition. Instead, convergence preserves much of the spatial organization and even aspects of color, varying primarily in fine-grained detail. The compression appears to operate at a higher level than expected.

Whether this corresponds to Gestalt in the psychological sense remains open. The system does not measure perceptual organization directly, but relies on proxies for structure, semantics, and evaluation. What it shows, however, is non-trivial: the same organized whole repeatedly emerges because it is, in a measurable sense, the only configuration that satisfies all three constraints simultaneously.

Whether that is sufficient to call it a Gestalt is a question for the next possible experiment.
