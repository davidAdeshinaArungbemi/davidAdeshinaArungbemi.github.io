The Interpretability Trap

Suzume · David Adeshina Arungbemi

A note added in October 2026: this is an early opinion piece. It doesn’t engage with the mechanistic interpretability research it argues against, so read it as a position, not a review of the field.

The Paradox of the Clueless Genius

Imagine you have a friend sitting beside you during exams. Somehow, most of the time, he passes all his math tests despite seeming completely clueless. You, the observer, are captivated. You wonder if your friend is secretly a genius, so you spend years of your life trying to decode the complexity of his reasoning.

Along the way, you find patterns. You notice correlations. You document behaviors. But eventually, a sinking feeling sets in: you realize you aren’t watching the mechanism of his mind at all.

The Ghosts of Shadows

You are like a creature trapped in a cave, studying shadows on a wall to understand what casts them. You’ve never seen the three-dimensional world, never seen the humans moving in the light. So, to explain your friend’s success, you invent elaborate theories — “ghosts of shadows,” you call them.

You build complex hierarchies of shadow-beings with shadow-logic. You map the patterns meticulously, finding that certain shadow movements predict others. You convince yourself you’re getting closer to the truth.

But at the end of your life, the revelation hits: you were studying projections of something you could never fully access. The shadows were real, and the patterns were real, but you were trapped in your two-dimensional plane, theorizing about a three-dimensional reality you couldn’t perceive. Even if your shadow theories predicted behavior, you were just modeling the surface of something fundamentally beyond your reach — something that operates on principles your shadow mind was never built to comprehend.

The Trap of Mechanistic Interpretability

This is the exact situation we face with Large Language Models.

Mechanistic interpretability is our attempt to map these shadows. It can reveal structures, patterns, and correlations inside the models, and it occasionally yields insights. But these architectures are inherently limited — they are pattern recognition systems at their core. No matter how sophisticated the analysis, the “why” behind their behavior remains inaccessible.

Whether your “dummy friend” passed because of hidden genius, luck, or because the teacher was lazy is ultimately unknowable if you cannot see inside his head. In the long run, trying to fully understand these models may be a misallocation of time and effort: productive in small doses, but fundamentally constrained.

Designing the Light

If we consider the alternative — building AI systems from first principles with interpretable reasoning structures — we change the paradigm. We can create architectures where understanding is not a puzzle to solve after the fact, but a property designed into the system.

Like how humans invented mathematics to reason clearly about the world, we can design AI whose behavior we can genuinely comprehend, extend, and trust.

The Takeaway

Mechanistic interpretability is valuable for what it can teach us, but we should be wary of overinvesting in reverse-engineering models whose foundations limit what can be understood. Our long-term focus should be on designing systems that allow comprehension from the outset, rather than chasing shadows that may never resolve into light.

Trying to fully understand something that works but has no underlying intelligible mechanism is a dangerous gamble. We need to stop marveling at the shadows on the wall and start building the light source ourselves.
