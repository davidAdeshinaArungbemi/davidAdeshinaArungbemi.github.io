The Narrative Hypothesis

Suzume · David Adeshina Arungbemi

What if the universe is a story?

Not metaphorically nor as a poetic comfort. What if narrative is the actual fabric — the mode in which reality was conceived, constructed, and is still unfolding?

I’ve been sitting with this question for a while now, and I can’t shake it. When I look at the night sky, at a sunset, at the strange elegance of a physical law — there’s something in it that feels authored.

Here’s the thread I’ve been pulling.

Science has evolved over centuries. Through the scientific method, through mathematics, through experimentation, we’ve built an extraordinary picture of reality. But there’s another tradition that has also evolved, in parallel. Storytelling. Narrative. We’ve developed theories of it, structures, grammars of meaning. It’s not less rigorous than science. It’s differently rigorous.

And here’s what I think we’ve missed: humans are narrative by nature. We don’t experience life as data. We experience it as a story unfolding, with characters, tension, meaning, consequence. From the earliest humans to now, that’s a global narrative. And within it, billions of smaller ones, interacting, colliding, merging, forming communities, cultures, civilisations.

This isn’t accidental. Consider, for a moment, the idea of a creator — an intelligence behind the design of everything. Most of the world’s traditions agree on at least this much. If we take that premise seriously, and if we are made in the image of that intelligence, then our narrative nature isn’t something we invented.

But even if you don’t, consider what we are made of. Stardust. Literally. The carbon in our bodies, the iron in our blood, forged in the cores of stars that lived and died before our sun existed. We are not separate from the universe, observing it from outside. We are it, looking at itself. And if the universe is narrative in nature, then what emerged from it, us, was always going to be narrative too. Not by accident, not by culture, but by composition.

The creator made the universe. The universe made us. And something of that original nature passed through every layer of that making, all the way down to the way we dream, remember, and explain.

Reality is a narrative. And so, inevitably, are we.

So how does this apply to science?

The scientific method is powerful. But it operates in one domain, the technical, the measurable, the falsifiable. What I’m proposing is that there’s another domain available to us. A narrative space. And that some problems might be easier to see, easier to solve, from inside it.

Think of it like the relationship between time domain and frequency domain in signal processing. Certain problems are extraordinarily difficult to solve in one domain and almost obvious in the other. The Fourier transform doesn’t change the underlying reality, it just reveals a different face of it. Narrative might be that kind of transform for scientific problems.

Here’s what that could look like in practice. Say we’ve solved part of a phenomenon but the rest remains out of reach. What if we mapped what we know into narrative space and then explored where the story could go? Not as fantasy, but as structured imagination. The solutions we find there, we carry back and test in the real world.

I know what the objection is. Narratives aren’t rigorous. They’re loose, subjective, unconstrained. And that’s true, which is why this isn’t about replacing the scientific method. It’s about having somewhere to go when the scientific method gets stuck. A place to generate possibilities that logic alone couldn’t reach.

But who does this exploring?

Not every scientist is a storyteller. And not every storyteller knows enough science to navigate it responsibly. That distance exists. But there’s a group of people who already live in it, science communicators. People whose job is to take the dense, technical, and often beautiful ideas of science and translate them into something a general audience can feel and understand.

What if we pushed that further? What if science communicators became the bridge, carrying the essential groundedness, the laws, the constraints, into creative spaces where authors, visual artists, musicians, and filmmakers could take them and explore?

I’m not talking about pure imagination with no anchor. The groundedness has to travel with the idea. The laws don’t disappear in narrative space but are reshaped. They become characters with motivations, tensions with consequences, structures with direction. And within those constraints, the creative mind does what it does best, finds paths that a purely analytical approach would never walk down.

A novelist might find the resolution to an unresolved arc that mirrors a physical phenomenon. A visual artist, through perception alone, might stumble onto a geometry that a mathematician hasn’t thought to look for. A composer might hear a pattern in a problem that no one has thought to listen for.

Perception is powerful. We underestimate it constantly.

Beyond Human.

This is where it gets interesting to me personally.

Imagine an AI capable of translating between these two domains. Not just generating stories, and not just solving equations, but genuinely mapping technical reality into narrative space, exploring it there, and returning with something a scientist could actually use.

I can’t tell you exactly how you’d train such a thing. But I can imagine what it would do. It would take an unsolved problem, the missing piece of a phenomenon, the gap in a theory, and recast it. Give it stakes. Give it direction. Let it run. And somewhere in that running, in that exploration of possible directions a story could go, it finds a path. Then it translates back.

What makes this more than fantasy is the constraint. The model isn’t free to invent. It carries the laws with it into narrative space, the way a ship carries its shape into water. The narrative bends around what is true. And that bending might reveal something.

But it doesn’t have to stop at writing. The AI could explore in visual art. It could generate images that carry the structure of a problem in a form a human eye might recognise before a human mind does. A researcher looks at a generated image and something clicks. Not because the image explained anything, but because it triggered something. Perception arriving before language.

That’s not a small thing. That might be the whole thing.

What I Wish For.

I want a world where the sciences and the arts are not two separate countries with a border between them. Where the beauty of a physical law can be expressed, felt, wondered at, not just solved. Where a painter and a physicist can sit at the same table and both leave with something they didn’t have before.

I want a world where human progress doesn’t move in a straight line but accelerates, where every hour we understand a little more, feel a little more, create a little more. Where the tools we’ve built, including AI, are turned not just toward efficiency but toward discovery and wonder.

I don’t know if the universe is literally a story. I can’t prove it. But I know that when I look at the night sky, something in me responds to it the way I respond to a great piece of art, with the feeling that there is more here than I can hold. That it means something. That it is going somewhere.

The Narrative Hypothesis is not a solution. It’s an invitation. To look at what we don’t yet understand and ask not just what it is, but what story it’s trying to tell.
