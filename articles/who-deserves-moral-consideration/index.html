Who Deserves Moral Consideration? I’m Not Sure Anymore.

Suzume · David Adeshina Arungbemi

I’m currently in the research track of Electric Sheep’s Futurekind Fellowship — a 12-week program at the intersection of AI and animal protection. And honestly, that’s probably why I’m writing this, because the programme has a way of pulling questions out of you before you have any answers ready.

Going in, the closest thing I had to a starting point was AI safety — and mostly from the human angle. I hadn’t really sat with the wider question of who or what deserves moral consideration in the first place.

Then the questions started piling up.

Who counts as conscious? How do you verify it? What if you can’t? What if verification is permanently impossible — not just hard, but structurally, fundamentally impossible? The fellowship pushed me into territory I wasn’t prepared for, and here we are. This is me trying to think out loud.

The Standard Approach Is Already Broken

Most of the time, when we ask “does this being deserve moral consideration?”, we’re really asking: does it have the right properties? Is it sentient? Can it suffer? Does it have preferences?

This sounds reasonable until you actually try to apply it.

Two philosophers — David Gunkel and Joshua Gellers — propose something different. Gunkel argues that instead of looking at properties, we should look at relationships. If you live with a robot, talk to it, depend on it, grieve when it breaks — that relationship itself carries moral weight, regardless of whether the robot “truly” feels anything inside.

Gellers goes even wider. Some entities don’t have obvious individual interests but still play a role in sustaining a larger system that matters. Think of a river. It doesn’t feel pain. But it sustains ecosystems, communities, entire ways of life. Its interests exist at the level of the whole, not the individual thing.

I find both of these compelling. Not because they’re philosophically airtight. Because they feel human — they start from where we actually are, from relationships and systems we can observe, rather than demanding we first solve consciousness. It’s the kind of thinking that meets you where you stand.

The Translation Problem

Here’s the thing that really got me thinking. We talk a lot about communicating with nonhuman animals — dolphins, primates, crows. And it’s genuinely exciting work. But I keep coming back to a smaller, weirder problem: human-to-human translation is already broken in ways we mostly ignore.

I remember watching a video about translators who work for world leaders. Sometimes, when a leader makes a joke, the translator just says “it was a joke” and laughs. They don’t even attempt the translation. The joke exists in a cultural context that doesn’t port over. So they preserve the social function of the moment instead of the content.

If that’s where we are between human languages — what hope do we have of accessing what it’s like to be a bat? Or an octopus? Or artificial life such as sentient AI?

And this is actually where Gunkel gets more interesting. If genuine verification of another’s inner life is impossible, then basing moral consideration on “confirmed sentience” is building on sand. You’d end up granting moral status only to minds similar enough to yours to be legible. That’s not a principled position. It’s familiarity bias.

Does Pain Even Exist the Same Way Everywhere?

It befuddles me.

There’s a distinction philosophers make between phenomenal pain — the actual “ouch,” the felt experience — and nociception, which is just a functional response to harmful stimuli. A bacterium moves away from toxins. Does it feel anything doing that? Almost everyone’s intuition says no. But notice — that intuition is just familiarity bias again. Bacteria are so unlike us that we dismiss the question before asking it.

Shrimp is genuinely contested. They have nociceptors. They guard injured limbs. They respond to painkillers. But whether there’s actually something it’s like to be a shrimp in pain — nobody knows. And the troubling part is that we may never know. Not because we lack data, but because the question might be unanswerable from the outside.

Then I thought about vertebrates. We can clearly see primates suffering — they cry, they shed tears, they show fear in ways we recognize immediately. Whales too. There’s something familiar there, something that maps onto our own experience enough to trigger a response in us. But that legibility is doing all the moral work. We’re not detecting suffering — we’re recognizing our own reflection.

The further you move from that reflection, the less certain everything becomes. And there’s no point on the spectrum where you can draw a clean line and say: consciousness starts here.

The Doll on the Floor

Here’s a thought experiment I came up with.

Say someone stabs a doll. We both agree the doll isn’t conscious. No harm done, right? But what if we didn’t know? Now we’re stuck asking: did it feel anything? Should there be consequences? We’d never know the answer. So how do we proceed?

I think we have to look at the human. Not the doll.

Stabbing is something humans associate with violence, with harm, with violation. Recreating that action — even on an object — reflects something about the person doing it, or at minimum, reinforces something in them. It’s not because the doll suffered, but because of what it does to the one holding the knife.

This isn’t a new idea. Aristotle was saying something like it thousands of years ago — that how we act shapes who we become. The act matters independently of the immediate harm caused.

This is why the rise of AI chatbots for entertainment worries me, honestly. Not just because of what it might mean for the AI but because of what it might cultivate in the people using them. We have the capacity to harm each other regardless of how kind we start out. There’s a reason the saying goes — evil communication corrupts good manners.

We Can’t Escape Our Own Perspective

This is the part that I found both the most frustrating and the most clarifying.

Every instrument we build to detect sentience was built by a sentient thing trying to find itself in the mirror. Behaviour? We bias toward behaviour we can read. Nervous system complexity? We set the threshold. Pain response? We defined what counts as pain. Evolutionary proximity? That’s just “similar to us” with extra steps.

The philosopher Thomas Nagel wrote a paper called “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” His point was simple and made me sad: you could study a bat’s brain exhaustively, read every paper ever written about echolocation, and still not know what it feels like from the inside to navigate the world that way. That inner texture — the subjectivity — only exists from the inside. You’re permanently on the outside.

And this doesn’t just apply to bats. It applies to other humans too. When someone’s in pain, we’re reading behaviours and hearing words. We’re not accessing the pain itself. Sometimes we get it wrong. Misunderstandings happen. People feel unseen.

Scaled up to fish, shrimp, an alien species, an AI — the gap doesn’t just get bigger. It may be unbridgeable.

Maybe That’s Okay

I spent a long time feeling like this was a problem to be solved. Like better technology or better philosophy would eventually close the gap.

I’m less sure about that now.

Maybe accepting the impossibility is actually the more honest place to stand. Not nihilistically — not “nothing matters so do whatever.” More like: we have always made moral decisions under uncertainty. We just pretended we had firmer ground than we did.

If that’s true, then the question is reshapen. It moves from “what have we confirmed about this being?” to “given that we can’t fully know — what kind of person do we want to be in our uncertainty?” I think that’s closer to Gunkel’s instinct than it looks. The relationship carries meaning even when the metaphysics stays unresolved.

I don’t have a tidy conclusion here. I don’t think one exists. But I’m increasingly convinced that the discomfort itself is worth something. The people who find these questions too easy to answer have usually stopped really looking at them.

I’m still looking.
