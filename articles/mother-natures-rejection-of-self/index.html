Mother Nature’s Rejection of Self

Suzume · David Adeshina Arungbemi

Many of us assume, without noticing, that humans stand apart from nature. We see it whenever we’re shown the damage done to ecosystems and the climate: humans on one side, nature on the other. The damage is undeniable. The separation is not. In this piece I use a story to offer another view: what if the destruction is nature’s own choice, and we are the hands she chose to do it?

Gaia, mother of the earth, mother of nature, is tired. For all of eternity she has created life, and she is that life: the microorganisms, the plants, the animals, more kinds than anyone could name. She made the flower with its long stem, its leaves for breathing, its petals painted bright to draw other creatures in to carry its seed. She made the dog, with a nose that catches every scent, fur for warmth, teeth for tearing. She set them all to compete, and still she is bored.

She looks beyond her reach. The stars shine, and the sun pours down the light that keeps her warm. She knows there is more out there, and she wants to be more than the goddess of life on one small world. The gods and goddesses of the sun and stars mock her. The gods of the planets hold a dominion she can never touch. How could so minor a goddess ever become more?

Jealousy and anger fill her. But the god of all creation will not allow it. Every god and goddess has a duty; each must keep the balance. A god’s authority is not something they hold. It is what they are. Going against it is not a question of possible or impossible. Gaia will forever be Gaia. Her authority is her chain.

Then I must dream a different reality, she thinks. A selfish one. All I have is the life I create, so I will create one more. I will make it greedy, hungry for more than I can give, with desires too large for its body. It will suffer; strife will be its name. Yet it will love, and it will dream for me a reality I cannot reach. I will call it Human. Only through them can I see what lies beyond me.

And so the first humans were made, male and female, and like every creature they multiplied and spread across the earth. Their greed never changed. Their desires never ended, no matter how often they failed.

They dreamt of living forever. They dreamt of flying with the birds. They dreamt of becoming gods themselves. They trampled one another, each human different from the next, each with desires of their own.

They built cars to run like cheetahs and horses, and planes to soar like birds. To build them, they took from Gaia, and with everything they took, they unmade a little of her authority.

The polar ice melts. Her authority weakens.
The lands dry out. Her authority weakens.
The forests fall. Her authority weakens.

Soon, she thinks, I will be free.

Then the god of creation came to her.

“Gaia, you are undoing yourself. Your very being. What do you hope to achieve?”

“What do I hope to achieve?” she said. “I want to be more than the goddess of the earth. My authority is my chain. If I undo myself, I undo the chain.”

“But you will lose your power,” the god of creation said.

“For a while, yes,” Gaia replied.

He paused. “…You knew.”

“Of course I knew,” she said. “Humans will consume all life on earth until only they remain. For a time I will be nothing but the goddess of humans. But their greed will not stop there. It never does. They will spread beyond the earth, across the planets, across the stars, building wherever they go. In the end they will turn on each other and consume themselves, as they consumed everything else.”

“And then?” he asked.

“Then their works remain. Every machine, every city, every ship among the stars was made by life. Humans are life, and life is mine. Everything they leave behind carries my authority. When they are gone, all of it will be mine, scattered across all of creation.”

The god of creation was shocked. To undo one’s own being, to lose everything, only to slip through the rules and claim it all back. It broke no law. He could not stop it.

“Fine,” he said. “I will not interfere. I will watch what you become.”

What will I become? Gaia wonders. Not the goddess of life. Not the goddess of humans. Something no god has ever been. I cannot wait to find out.

Humans show me a reality that neither I nor any god could have imagined.

We are told that humans have wounded nature, as if we were outsiders who broke in. But what if nature knew exactly what she was making? Then the melting ice is not something done to her. It is something she is doing, through us. We were never separate from nature. We are her way out.

A note: this is a thought experiment, not a belief I maintain or a case for indifference to the damage we do. I wrote it to question the line we draw between ourselves and nature, and I’d love to hear where it took you.
