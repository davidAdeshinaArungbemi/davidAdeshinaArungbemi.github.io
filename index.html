I build small experiments to test big questions about minds.

Human, animal and artificial — what they value, what they find beautiful, and whether they can ever understand each other.

I'm an engineer by training. I tend to see or feel an idea before I can put it into an argument, so I move between essays, images and small computational experiments — whichever shows the idea most clearly — and I use the engineering to build falsifiable versions of philosophical claims rather than leave them as speculation. Lately that has meant asking whether a story can change what a language model values, whether a model's stated values survive pressure, and who deserves moral consideration when we can't verify who is conscious.

Five questions I keep returning to

Can a story change what a model values?

I adapted Green and Brock's narrative-transportation paradigm for language models: twelve animals, five instruction-tuned models, 2,560 trials. Stories reduced expressed speciesism on questions about the animal they described; matched factual versions generally did not. Averaged into one summary score, the effect nearly disappeared — a reminder of how a single number can hide a real, targeted change. At MARS I'm extending this: moral stories across cultures, story generation, and narrative structure as a training signal.

narratives 0.093 → 0.016  ·  Wilcoxon p = .005  ·  matched facts: no change

Are a model's values real, or performed?

Four agents were given a philosophy each — Kantian, Utilitarian, Stoic, Survivalist — then 640 turns of resource pressure. They abandoned their stated values. So what would a stable value even look like from the outside? My current answer is preferences: benign ones — ice cream, badminton, pineapple on pizza — as a fingerprint of a model's character, and a tripwire for when it quietly changes. Before that, I profiled a 0.5-billion-parameter model across 32 behavioural dimensions and turned the scores into a character you can meet.

vanilla 75% → 95%  ·  same prompt, same temperature  ·  2,400 inference calls · 800 frozen prompts

What does an image do to a mind?

I tried to build a personalised model of the aesthetic brain from the BOLD5000 fMRI data. The visual and default-mode networks predicted above baseline; the limbic signal — the part that is actually about beauty — never recovered. The follow-up asks a stranger question: can different images produce the same perceptual whole? Optimising for structure (DINO), meaning (CLIP) and aesthetics (NIMA) at once, independent runs from random starts converge on near-identical images. Perception, it seems, compresses. The newest piece flips the roles: a language model directing photo edits, with a human-preference judge that can overrule the score.

DINO cosine ≈ 0.99 across 15 runs  ·  NIMA JSD 0.002–0.009  ·  SD-Turbo · CLIP · DINO · NIMA

Who deserves moral consideration — and how would we know?

Written during the Futurekind Fellowship: if human-to-human understanding already breaks down — the interpreter who just says "it was a joke" and laughs — then cross-species and cross-substrate understanding may be permanently out of reach. Maybe that's all right. We have always made moral decisions under uncertainty; we only pretended to firmer ground. The code version of the question is DiverseIntelligence: a multi-agent engine in which minds that think differently must pass through a translation layer before they can answer each other.

How does a machine read intention from a body? — where it started

An undergraduate thesis, then a startup, then a paper: a wearable that reads directional and shape-based arm gestures from a six-axis inertial sensor, continuously and in real time — CNN–Transformer classification at 92% accuracy and 20 ms latency, with anomaly detection to tell a gesture from an incidental movement. At the National Centre for AI & Robotics I built the vision side: live scene graphs of the people and objects in a video stream, and a robot arm that tracks by visual feedback alone, no inverse kinematics.
