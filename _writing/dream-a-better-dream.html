In this piece, I explore what epistemology is, what I mean by visual epistemology, and a speculative future where we search for truth and meaning through visuals.

A quick note before we begin: I’m not a philosopher. I’d call myself an enthusiast. This is my current understanding, and it will change as I learn more.

It’s also been a while since I read Art Theory as Visual Epistemology, edited by Harald Klinke. It’s a collection of essays on visual epistemology, mixing art history and philosophy, so please read it for a more rigorous take on that side. The rest, especially my dream for the future, is my own inspirational and speculative thinking.

What Is Epistemology?

In simple terms, epistemology is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature of knowledge. What is knowledge? How do you know that you know something? Why does science give us knowledge?

Here’s a puzzle. Imagine I dream the final answer to an unsolved problem in mathematics. I have no idea how to get there step by step. I just “know,” or “feel,” or “deeply believe” that this is the answer. Years later, someone solves the problem and arrives at the same answer.

Did I have knowledge? Or was it just a belief that happened to be true?

A classic answer in philosophy is that knowledge is justified true belief. Believing something isn’t enough, and it being true isn’t enough either. You also need good reasons, such as evidence or proof. By that standard, my dream wasn’t knowledge. It was a true belief without justification.

But knowledge isn’t the only way we make sense of the world. That brings me to understanding, and to visuals, especially art.

Knowledge vs. Understanding

What’s the difference between knowledge and understanding? A common way to put it: knowledge is having the facts, while understanding is seeing what those facts mean and how they relate to each other.

I want to focus on that relational part. But I’d go one step further. I believe understanding connects not only facts, but beliefs too. We make sense of things by linking what we see to everything we already know and believe.

To show what I mean, let’s step into a story.

A Thought Experiment: The Premonition

You are in ancient Nazareth, walking through the village, looking for something to eat.

You notice a young boy learning his father’s trade. Joseph, the carpenter, is teaching his son how to work with wood. The boy is still small, still learning.

Then he brings the hammer down the wrong way.

He cries out. Blood wells from the centres of his palms.

His mother sees him and runs toward him, dropping to her knees.

“Jesus!”

The neighbours turn to watch.

Earlier, Joseph had propped two pieces of wood against each other to shade the work area from the harsh sun. From where you stand, they form a strange shape.

A cross.

And Jesus is standing right at its centre.

The coincidence catches your attention. Then you notice something else.

He had clasped his wounded hands together to contain the pain. But as his mother reaches for him, he lifts them slightly, opening them into a small V.

“I’m fine,” he says, trying to reassure her.

But you can still see the blood. A single drop hangs from each hand, and both hands rest directly over the shadow of the cross.

You stare. Something about the scene unsettles you. You know what the Romans do to criminals. You know what crosses mean.

For a moment, you imagine the impossible.

Could that be his fate?

You turn to the man beside you.

“Do you see that?”

“See what?”

“The cross. His hands. The blood.”

The man looks at the boy, then back at you.

“You’re insane.”

Perhaps you are. But the thought stays with you.

Years pass. You grow old.

Then one day, while visiting Jerusalem, you hear gossip spreading through the streets. A Roman execution is about to take place.

“The carpenter’s son,” a woman whispers. “They say they’re crucifying him.”

You stop walking. The words strike you like a hammer.

You make your way to the execution site. And there they are.

Three crosses. Three men. And at the centre, Jesus.

The boy you saw all those years ago is now a man in his thirties, nailed to a cross. His hands pierced. His fate exactly as you had imagined it.

You were right.

You foresaw the crucifixion of Jesus Christ.

What Just Happened? Visual Understanding

How did you get it right? Surely it was just a coincidence. I’d call it visual understanding, and it happens a lot in art.

In that scene, you observed separate details: the wound, the wood, the hands, the shadow. You connected each one to things you already knew or believed. And from those connections, a feeling emerged. A sense of foreshadowing.

That feeling wasn’t knowledge, at least not in the empirical sense. There was no evidence that Jesus would be crucified. Crucifixion was for criminals, and nothing suggested he would ever commit a crime. What you saw had nothing to do with evidence, and yet you arrived somewhere.

It didn’t come in words. It came through the relations between what you saw and what you knew and believed.

That is subjective understanding. After all, you were the only one who noticed it. No one else felt it. Like art, it was personal.

My Dream for the Future

What’s Left for Us?

Many AI models today are trained on images and can even spot symbolism in a scene. But as far as I know, they don’t have this kind of understanding: a felt, personal meaning that no one else around you sees. I wish they did; it would be fascinating.

These models are now used everywhere: at work, in automating tasks, even in research. So what’s left for us?

Humans as Dreamers

I believe we become a kind of overseer. Not in the sense of handing a person full control of an AI, but as dreamers. People who dream a better future. People who have premonitions of what should happen next, or who dream up the structure of a new chemical compound, then communicate that to the AI and discover new things along the way. It has happened before: the chemist August Kekulé said the ring shape of benzene came to him in a daydream of a snake biting its own tail.

I imagine an interaction between humans and AI that starts with visuals, but isn’t really about them. For me, an image is a doorway. When I look at a painting or a scene, something deep within me stirs. A meaning forms that I can feel before I can explain it, and sometimes I can’t explain it at all.

The philosopher Michael Polanyi put it this way: “we can know more than we can tell.” That’s the space I want to explore with AI. Not a visual space exactly, but a space of meaning, and images are how we reach it.

A kind of symbiosis. AI handles the complex, nitty-gritty work. We bring the skill of observing, the way we look at a painting: noticing relations, feeling what they stir in us, finding our own subjective meaning, and passing that meaning on.

Because there’s no equation for a better tomorrow. There are countless paths we could take, and we’ve already explored many of them through fiction. Looking at art, writing and film, I’ve come to realise that we humans carry something exceptional: the ability to create meaning and shape our reality. That’s something I want to do. I think it’s something we should become.

Dream a Better Dream

I once watched a scene from The Adventures of Sharkboy and Lavagirl (2005), a film by Robert Rodriguez, the director of Spy Kids.

In it, Max, a lonely ten-year-old, escapes into Planet Drool, a dream world he invented and filled with his own heroes. But the dream world starts falling apart. At its lowest point, Lavagirl, one of his heroes, sacrifices herself to save him.

Broken, Max faces a hard truth. He wanted all his dreams to come true, but he only ever dreamt for himself. He used his dreams to escape the real world, when the real world needed his help. Selfish dreams, he realises, shouldn’t come true.

So what do you do when your dreams have been destroyed? The answer is simple:

Dream a better dream.

An unselfish one. Max does exactly that. By the end of the film, he tells his class to dream a better dream, then work to make it real.

So I’ll keep dreaming of a better tomorrow, of a future I want to see come true. I’ll try. I just don’t know how yet, and I think that’s true for many of us.

Uncertain, but still dreaming.

“Dream a better dream.”
