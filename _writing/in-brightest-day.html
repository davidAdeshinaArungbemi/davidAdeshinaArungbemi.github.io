“In brightest day, in blackest night, no evil shall escape my sight. Let those who worship evil’s might beware my power…Green Lantern’s light!”

Yes, Green Lantern.

The oath draws a line: light on one side, darkness on the other, with evil somewhere in the dark, kept out by our constant vigilance. But Green Lantern didn’t invent that line. Long before superheroes, people were already putting moral weight on the sun and the dark — as if darkness were not merely the absence of light, but evidence of something hiding inside it.

This is an attempt to trace where it comes from— and then to deliberately pull it apart. What happens to “the evil-one” if there’s no night left to hide in? What if the familiar divide between day and night stretches into something as slow and strange as the changing seasons? And what if, somewhere along the way, we realise we had it backwards all along?

Language, Biology & Culture

Language carries the association almost everywhere. Across unrelated languages and cultures, light tends to be associated with happiness, virtue, knowledge, and safety, while darkness carries associations of unhappiness, deceit, ignorance, and moral badness. The pattern is striking because it isn’t confined to one religion or one civilisation. The Pythagoreans placed light and darkness beside good and evil in their famous pairs of opposites. Genesis begins creation by separating light from darkness. In Egyptian myth, Ra spent each night fighting Apophis through the underworld so that the sun could rise again. Zoroastrianism made the opposition between light and darkness part of its cosmic architecture. The stories differ enormously, but they keep producing the same character: something hostile to order that belongs on the dark side. The evil-one isn’t a comic-book invention. He is a role that human cultures have been casting, independently, for a very long time.

You can see traces of such divide in biology. Part of this is just the basic physics of staying alive— daylight grows the food, and without it we starve. But the stranger evidence is written into biology itself. Most mammals are nocturnal; only around a fifth of species, humans included, are built to be active by day.

Maybe our association of darkness with danger isn’t only cultural or biology. There may have been a very practical evolutionary reason for humans to fear the dark: for most of our history, the animals hunting us could see better in it than we could. Fear of darkness, then, would have been less a superstition than a survival instinct.

Then fire came in: our own day in the night. Once early humans gained lasting control of it, night stopped being pure dead time. Anthropologist Polly Wiessner’s research among the Ju’hoansi of the Kalahari found that firelight did more than keep predators at bay; it changed what people talked about. Daytime conversation centred on practicalities — food, work, complaints. Around the fire, conversation turned to stories, memories, and the old times. Fire didn’t just reclaim a few hours of night. It created a space in which those hours could become something else: story, memory, and, perhaps, culture itself.

Modern crime data complicates the story. More crime overall happens during the day: burglars want an empty house, and houses are empty while people are at work, not asleep. But some of the crimes we instinctively associate with “evil” — murder, robbery, assault — do cluster more heavily after dark, particularly in the evening and around midnight. Darkness, then, may not be the natural habitat of crime so much as the cultural habitat of a certain kind of violence: the kind that depends on vulnerability, isolation, and fear. The old instinct may not have been entirely wrong. It may simply have been describing a narrower category than crime itself.

Three Thought Experiments

If day and night are this deeply wired into how we picture good and evil, what happens if the frame comes apart?

In Brightest Day.

Say the sun never sets — and, further, that we no longer need sleep, so even the biological argument for night disappears. Evil doesn’t stop existing; it simply loses its oldest hiding place. A thief can’t rely on darkness in a world where everything is always visible. So where does he go?

He assimilates.

Unable to hide in shadow, he hides in plain sight, inside the roles society already trusts: the merchant who quietly overcharges, the official who expects a small “favour” before doing his job. Nobody is looking for a thief because, on the surface, there is no thief to find. There is only a merchant doing what merchants do, an official doing what officials do, a system operating exactly as expected. The darkness is gone, but the hiding place remains.

Take away the dark, and evil doesn’t disappear. It learns to look ordinary.

In Gentlest Night, In Fiercest Day

Imagine a culture that experiences night as the gentle one: quiet, cool, free of the day’s demands, lit softly by moon and stars instead of a sun harsh enough to hurt the eyes. Day, by contrast, is the dangerous one — hot, exhausting, and, crucially, the time when everyone can see you.

So where does the evil-one go? He cannot stay in a night the culture has decided is safe. He is pushed into daylight — but daylight is public, and an obvious thief still gets caught. So he stops being obvious. As in “In Brightest Day,” he becomes the merchant who overcharges because “that’s just business,” the official who expects a bribe because “that’s just how things work.”

Evil doesn’t vanish under scrutiny. It becomes ordinary. It becomes the quiet tax everyone pays and eventually stops noticing. Night, meanwhile, is where people finally get to rest from it.

In Goldest Spring, In Ashen Winter

Zoom the same split out from a 24-hour cycle to a yearly one, and winter takes the place night once held. In agricultural societies, it was when crops stopped, illness rose, and food grew scarce — an easy place to locate “evil.” Narnia’s White Witch is the fictional version of this: a winter that refuses to end, permanently overdue for a spring that never comes.

Even the language shifts with it. “While men slept, his enemy came” becomes something closer to “while men shivered, his enemy came” — both sleep and shivering marking vulnerability. But endless winter is not a moral inversion. It is simply death. Crops fail; water freezes. A night ends with morning. Winter promises the same, but when that promise is withheld, the season stops being a backdrop and becomes a condition.

Conclusion

Run all three experiments and the same thing happens: evil doesn’t actually need darkness. It needs somewhere unwatched, something obscured, or something familiar enough that nobody thinks to question it. Darkness was just the oldest, easiest version of that cover — written into language and myth long before we had words for corruption, bureaucracy, or a bribe that “everyone” pays.

Technology has spent centuries chipping away at that older framework — streetlights, cameras, satellites, screens. It’s harder now, in a literal sense, for things to hide in the dark. But visibility isn’t the same as recognition. A corrupt official can take a bribe at 2 p.m. with the blinds open. A merchant can exploit someone in a brightly lit shop. A doctor can save a life at 3 a.m. and discriminate against a patient at noon. The question was never really whether an act could be seen. It’s whether anyone sees what’s wrong with it — and that, not darkness, is where the evil-one hides now: not in shadow, but in normality.

Which means the Green Lantern’s task has changed. It’s no longer enough to shine a light into the dark. The harder task is learning what to look at when everything is already illuminated — and noticing what everyone else has quietly learned not to see.
