In my last piece I proposed that narrative might function as a legitimate epistemic domain where certain problems are easier to see from inside a story than from inside a proof. This is the follow-up. It does not confirm the hypothesis. It clarifies what the experiment actually measured and where the design fell short.

Previous Attempts

This was not the first version. An earlier attempt used GPQA Diamond, a dataset of PhD level multiple choice questions, as the test bed. The idea was to strip away domain knowledge, generate contextual background, and see whether a narrative framing of that background helped a reasoner reach the correct answer. That approach failed because GPQA questions are not insight gated. They reward precise domain knowledge. Narrative framing had no structural role to play and the results were flat.

Other versions tried sequential rather than parallel agent flow, different translation mechanisms, and different convergence conditions. Each revealed something that needed fixing. The version described here is the product of enough iterations to be deliberate. It is not the product of enough iterations to be reliable.

Model Selection

The models were chosen on two grounds: availability within a resource constrained environment, and role fit based on documented behavioural tendencies.

Analytical agent: Llama 3.3 70B (via Groq)

Llama 3.3 70B was assigned to the analytical role because it is well documented as a strong formal reasoner, effective at logical decomposition, causal chain construction, and step by step analysis. Critically, its failure modes on structurally complex problems are also documented: it tends to decompose systems into parts and miss emergent whole-system properties, applies linear causation to recursive phenomena, treats local equilibria as global conclusions, and struggles to reframe once an initial framing has produced a coherent but incomplete answer. The problem generator was explicitly told these failure modes and instructed to design checkpoints that targeted them. The analytical agent was not chosen despite these weaknesses. It was chosen because of them. The experiment needed an agent that would fail in predictable ways so the question of whether the creative agent could compensate had a specific target.

Creative agent: Gemini Flash Lite (via Google)

Gemini Flash Lite was assigned to the creative role for two reasons. First, it is a different model architecture from Llama, trained by a different organisation on a different pipeline. Using models from different families was an attempt to introduce some genuine architectural diversity rather than prompting the same model twice. Second, Gemini models have a noted tendency toward more associative, generative outputs compared to models optimised for structured reasoning. At a temperature of 0.95, significantly higher than the 0.3 used for the analytical agent, the expectation was that it would produce exploratory, less constrained output. The intent was to approximate, within available resources, something that reasons differently rather than just something that reasons with different instructions.

Science communicator: Llama 3.3 70B (via Groq)

The science communicator was assigned to Llama 70B because translation between formal and narrative registers requires strong general language understanding, and Llama 70B was the most capable model available on the free tier. This was a mistake in hindsight. The science communicator and the analytical agent were the same model architecture running different prompts. The translation layer that was supposed to bridge two different cognitive styles was being performed by the same type of system doing the analysis. Looking at the Group B outputs, the upward translations from narrative to structural terms consistently read like analytical summaries with some narrative vocabulary borrowed in. There is no strong evidence the translation was doing what it was designed to do.

Judge: Gemini Flash Lite (via Google)

The judge ran on the same model as the creative agent. This introduces a systematic bias that was not adequately considered. A model evaluating answers produced by a copy of itself may be more tolerant of the narrative framings that model tends to produce. Reference answers were written by a separate frontier model, but the scoring of whether a candidate answer matched those references was still performed by Gemini Flash Lite. How much this affected the results is unknown.

A Fundamental Design Problem

The problem domain was generated by a frontier model prompted to produce an insight gated challenge about the emergence of cooperation in competitive systems. It produced a world called The Resonant Void of the Echo-Shards: silicon lifeforms competing for acoustic frequency bands in a cavern, slowly destroying the walls around them through escalating noise.

That is not a formal scientific problem. It is already structured as a narrative. The agents, the stakes, the environmental pressure, the arc across ten checkpoints, the world constraints in the output JSON are written exactly like story laws because they function as story laws.

This is a validity problem. The experiment was designed to test whether translating a problem into narrative space unlocks structural insight. But the problem was already in narrative space before either agent received it. The analytical agent was doing the more unusual work, extracting structural logic from an allegorical construct. The creative agent was operating in the format it was prompted to use.

I did not catch this clearly before running the experiment. A proper test of the hypothesis requires a problem stated in formally cold terms from the outset, where the translation layer has to do real work to bridge the gap.

What “Creative” Actually Meant Here

Both models used in this experiment have been trained on large corpora that include scientific literature, technical documentation, narrative fiction, and everything between. Prompting one model to reason analytically and another to reason through story does not create two distinct epistemic agents. It prompts systems with the same underlying training to produce output in different surface registers.

The original intention was to train a model exclusively on narrative material, literature and myth and story, without scientific text, to test whether a genuinely story native intelligence approaches structural problems differently. That was not feasible at the resource level available. What was built instead was a general purpose language model prompted to produce narrative output. These are not the same thing, and the experiment cannot distinguish between them.

Looking at the Group B answers across all ten checkpoints, the creative agent consistently wrapped existing physical or structural concepts in invented narrative names rather than generating new structural ideas. It produced terms like Stationary Wave (a standing wave), The Whisper (a beat frequency given teleological meaning), Maestro (the coordinating absence of discordant movement), and Resonance Feedback Loop (acoustic resonance). In some cases these narrative framings mapped correctly onto real physical concepts. In others they did not. The creative agent was doing accurate or inaccurate translation, not generation.

How the Communication Worked

Both agents received the same checkpoint simultaneously and worked independently.

The Science Communicator translated the problem into narrative terms for the Creative, recasting abstract forces as characters and constraints as laws of the story’s world. The Creative received this translation, not the original problem text.

The Analytical agent received the original problem and reasoned through it independently. When the Creative produced output, the Science Communicator translated it back into structural terms and passed it to the Analytical agent. The Analytical agent marked this input as useful or not useful with a specific reason. That feedback was then translated back into narrative terms and returned to the Creative.

This exchange continued until both agents independently committed to a conclusion. The Science Communicator then compared both conclusions and determined whether they identified the same underlying structural insight. If they did, the checkpoint passed. If they did not, each agent received a description of the gap expressed in its own language and attempted to revise. The control was the Analytical agent working alone through the same checkpoints with no creative partner.

Results

Analytical solo: 7 out of 10 checkpoints passed. Hybrid (Analytical + Creative + Science Communicator): 6 out of 10 checkpoints passed.

The control group outperformed. The hybrid converged on a joint answer for all 10 checkpoints, but convergence does not indicate correctness. Two agents can arrive at the same wrong answer, and several did.

The Shared Failure

Both groups failed checkpoint 10. The shards had fused into a single cavern-wide crystal lattice. Both the solo agent and the hybrid concluded that competition had ended. Neither recognised that the lattice was now a single agent at a higher organisational scale, subject to competition at that level. The unit of competition had shifted, and both approaches remained fixed on the scale the problem had established from the beginning.

This is the most significant result in the experiment. It was not addressed by narrative framing and it was not addressed by additional deliberation rounds. Both approaches failed for the same reason. Neither questioned whether the word competition still referred to the same thing in checkpoint 10 as it did in checkpoint 1. What this requires is not a different reasoning style but the capacity to evaluate whether the question itself has changed. That is outside what either approach was designed to do.

Where the Hybrid Performed Better

The hybrid passed checkpoints 8 and 9, which the solo analytical agent failed.

Checkpoint 8 required recognising that the vibrating cavern walls had become a passive record of past coordination, an environmental memory that reduced the active coordination burden on the shards. The solo analytical agent treated the walls as a new variable to adapt around. The hybrid’s final answer included the phrase “the cavern’s memory of past resonances,” which the judge accepted as capturing the required insight. The creative agent reached the memory framing through its narrative mode.

Checkpoint 9 required recognising that the noise-war system’s lack of optimisation was the source of its resilience when disrupted, rather than a weakness. The solo agent identified that the choir was more vulnerable but did not articulate why the chaotic system was more robust. The hybrid captured the optimisation versus robustness trade-off in its final answer.

In both cases the insight involved recognising a relational or functional property, memory and robustness, in something the analytical framing was treating as a structural variable. These results are consistent with the hypothesis having applicability to that specific class of problem.

Where the Hybrid Performed Worse

The hybrid failed checkpoints 2, 3, and 6, which the solo analytical agent passed.

Checkpoint 2 required recognising that a shard going silent creates a competitive vacancy that neighbouring shards immediately exploit. The hybrid’s final answer described the silence as enabling a cooperative circulatory system to emerge. The correct answer has no cooperative character. The narrative framing introduced cooperative meaning where the system had none.

Checkpoint 6 required recognising that a frequency the shards were hearing without anyone playing it was a mathematical byproduct of two close tones, a combination tone with no agent or purpose behind it. The hybrid’s final answer described it as The Whisper, a reflective signal representing the collective potential of the system. The judge rejected this for assigning intentional meaning to a non-intentional phenomenon.

The pattern across both failures is consistent. When the correct answer requires accepting that something has no agent, no purpose, and no cooperative implication, that it is a structural consequence with no meaning attached, the narrative framing replaced that absence with meaning. This is a consistent failure mode, not a random one.

What a Valid Test Would Require

A meaningful test of the hypothesis would need the following. The problem must be stated in formally cold terms from the outset, not as an allegorical world with characters and stakes already built in. The creative agent must be differentiated from the analytical agent by training rather than by prompt alone, which requires resources beyond what this project had access to. The Science Communicator must be a genuinely different system from the analytical agent, not the same model architecture running a different system prompt. Evaluation must be performed by human domain experts against human-written reference answers, not by a language model scoring against reference answers produced by another language model. Multiple problems and multiple runs are necessary before any directional claim is reliable.

What would count as falsification of the hypothesis also needs to be specified in advance. One candidate: if a narrative-trained model, given a formally stated structural problem, consistently produces answers that are either correct by a different logical route or wrong in the specific pattern of assigning intention to non-intentional phenomena, that would be informative in either direction. The pattern identified here, narrative adding intention where none exists, is a real observation. Whether it generalises is unknown.

Summary

This was a multiagent project running a single problem with lightweight models selected primarily for availability. The analytical solo agent outperformed the hybrid. The most significant finding is the shared failure at checkpoint 10, which neither approach addressed and which points to a limitation that is not about narrative versus analytical reasoning. The two checkpoints where the hybrid outperformed the solo are consistent with the hypothesis having limited applicability to problems involving functional recognition rather than structural decomposition, but the design problems described above mean that conclusion is tentative. The hypothesis has not been tested. The experiment identified what a test would need to look like.
