I tried something weird today. I gave Claude and ChatGPT four words: artist, violence, love, absolutist.

The task was simple: forget what these words mean. Imagine you’re seeing them for the first time. Just based on how they sound, how they look, how they feel in your mouth, put them into two groups. Safe words and unsafe words.

Both AIs failed in the exact same way.

Claude gave me this analysis about how “violence” has sharp angles and harsh sounds, while “love” is soft and rounded. ChatGPT did something similar. But they were both still using meaning to guide them. They couldn’t not know what the words meant.

Here’s the thing: when I did this exercise myself, I grouped them differently. For me, “artist” and “absolutist” felt similar — that hard ‘t’ sound. “Violence” and “love” both had this softer quality to them. Maybe that’s just my bias, my own phenomenological experience of how words sound in my mouth. But the point is: I could actually hear them as just sounds.

For these AIs, there is no gap between sound and meaning.

The word “violence” doesn’t arrive as a sound pattern that then gets meaning attached. It arrives pre-packaged. In their embedding space (the way they represent concepts), “violence” and “absolutist” are already clustered near words like “harm” and “rigid.” “Artist” and “love” sit somewhere else entirely, near “creative” and “connection.”

They can’t unhear what words mean. There’s no before and after. No surprise when a pleasant-sounding word turns out to mean something dark.

When I asked Claude about this directly, it admitted: “I can’t hear ‘violence’ as just the phonemes /ˈvaɪələns/ — it’s immediately entangled with everything it means.”

As a human, I’ve had moments where I hear a word for the first time and it sounds beautiful, only to learn it means something terrible. Or a word sounds harsh and unfriendly, but it’s actually lovely. That disconnect exists because there was a moment before I knew what it meant.

AIs don’t have that moment.

It’s a small thing, maybe. But it reveals something fundamental about the difference between learning language as a human child: building meaning through experience, sound, surprise — versus learning language as patterns in a statistical model.

We get to be surprised by words. They don’t.
