The question I would bring

Your GLoW project builds AI around a global workspace: specialist latent spaces translated into a shared one, so that information can be grounded across modalities and chained into reasoning. Anthropic’s July paper argues that language models already have something like it, and reads it with the Jacobian lens. I train language models, and I have used that lens in my own experiments.

My results touch the distinction at the heart of Global Workspace Theory, between what reaches report and what acts without it. One model learned to describe a reasoning process, and wrote its words into its reasoning, yet its decisions barely changed. Another changed its decisions after training on a story it never mentioned in its reasoning. I would like to study this with you: in your architectures, where the workspace is built in, and in language models, where it has to be found. What does training put into the workspace, and what acts from outside it?

Work that bears on it

A story that acts without being named MARS · 2026

I trained a small model (Gemma 4) on a fable written as its own answers about itself. Its decisions shifted the way they do when the fable is given in the prompt: on MACHIAVELLI it took the deceptive option 33.2% of the time, against 41.1% before training and 37.5% after training on an unrelated control text. No reasoning trace named the fable.

Is the story in the workspace? MARS · 2026

With Anthropic’s Jacobian lens I looked for the fable inside that model. When the fable was in context, the lens found it plainly: just before the answer it leaned toward the fable’s moral about twelve times as much as in the base model. In the trained model the moral’s signal was matched by the control, so it came from the training, not the fable.

Describing a reasoning process versus using it MARS · 2026

I trained the same kind of model on first-person accounts of a reasoning process, Mark Turner’s projection from The Literary Mind. It then described the process almost every time it was asked and used its vocabulary in up to two thirds of its reasoning, yet its decisions barely changed. Whether it learned the process or only its words is still open.

Predicting the brain’s response to an image 2026

I built a model that predicts fMRI responses (BOLD5000) to new images from their features, personalised from a person’s ranked image history, and an app that tunes generative art to raise the prediction. The predictions were weak overall and best in visual areas; my write-up analyses why.

A contrastive embedding that tells stories from technical writing, as a first step toward translating between them; and DiverseIntelligence, in progress, where AI agents playing Feynman, Kierkegaard and William James work on one problem while a translator rewrites each message into terms the listener already knows.
